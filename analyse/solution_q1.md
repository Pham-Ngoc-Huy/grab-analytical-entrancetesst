## Q1. Based on deep behavioural and P&L data, do you agree?

**Direct Answer: I do not agree with the Country Head's assessment.**

While the campaign delivered a 45% top-line GMV growth, user-level transaction data indicates that this is not a genuine "behavioral change" or the formation of a sustainable "cross-service habit." The growth surge is primarily a mechanical, incentive-driven response from users exploiting the promotion, rather than organic service adoption.

Here is the data-backed evidence refuting the habit-formation hypothesis:

* **Thin, subsidy-dependent acquisition:** New Mart Users accounted for a mere **13.24%** of the total Promo Mart user base and contributed just **13.4%** to the overall Mart GMV. More importantly, **100%** of these new users only transacted using the cross-sell voucher. We are essentially "buying" short-term transactions rather than stimulating genuine, underlying demand.
* **Distorted consumption behavior (Order Splitting):** Tracking a fixed cohort of 4,985 existing users reveals patterns that directly contradict natural consumption growth. The number of orders per user skyrocketed by **+135.3%**, yet the Average Order Value (AOV) actually declined by **-9.2%**. Customers are not consuming more food; they are deliberately splitting large orders into smaller ones to farm as many vouchers as possible.
* **Threshold Manipulation:** The volume of GrabFood orders specifically hitting the qualifying requirement (`item_subtotal ≥ 100K`) surged by a massive **+162%**. Furthermore, **5.8%** of tracked users actively stretched their historical basket sizes from `< 100K` to just cross the `≥ 100K` mark.

**Conclusion:**
The 45% GMV growth creates an illusion of success. The campaign did not establish a sustainable cross-platform habit. Instead, it created a mechanical loophole, effectively paying our existing users to change *how* they transact (to hunt for vouchers) rather than genuinely adopting a new lifestyle habit within the Grab ecosystem.
